# Theory conventions and checklists

This file sets conventions and checklists. It is not a textbook. It exists so that labels stay consistent and checks don't get skipped. If the student names a textbook or a teacher's convention, follow theirs, and say where it differs from what is below.

## Contents

## 1. Conventions that vary between textbooks

Ask which textbook their course uses when the answer depends on it. Common ones: Aldwell & Schachter (*Harmony and Voice Leading*), Kostka & Payne (*Tonal Harmony*), Laitz (*The Complete Musician*), Clendinning & Marvin (*The Musician's Guide to Theory and Analysis*), Caplin (*Classical Form*), and the free *Open Music Theory*. Some points where they differ:

- **Cadential six-four**: labeled `V⁶₄–⁵₃` (a dominant-function embellishment, the Schenkerian-influenced view) or `I⁶₄–V` (a tonic chord in second inversion). Textbooks, and even editions of the same textbook, differ. Both are taught. Say which one you are using.
- **Uppercase and lowercase numerals**: most books use case for chord quality (V, ii, vii°). Some analytical traditions use uppercase only, with quality shown separately.
- **Applied chords**: `V⁷/V` vs. "secondary dominant" vs. "tonicization". The terms differ, but the symbol `V⁷/V` is near-universal.
- **Augmented sixths**: `It⁺⁶`, `Fr⁺⁶`, `Ger⁺⁶`, sometimes without the plus sign.
- **Phrase and form terms**: the Caplin terms (sentence: presentation + continuation; period: antecedent + consequent) vs. older terms (parallel/contrasting period, phrase group).
- **Tenor range and part-writing strictness** vary between courses. Treat their course's rules as the rules.

## 2. Labels and symbols

- **Pitch**: scientific pitch notation, C4 = middle C. Spell with real accidentals: B♭3, F♯5.
- **Intervals**: quality + size: P5, M3, m6, A4, d5, d7. Compound intervals either as compound (M10) or reduced (M3), and say which.
- **Roman numerals**: case shows quality. I, ii, iii, IV, V, vi, vii° in major. i, ii°, III, iv, V, VI, vii° in minor (with the raised leading tone). `°` diminished, `ø` half-diminished, `+` augmented.
- **Inversion figures**: triads root (none), first inversion `⁶`, second inversion `⁶₄`. Seventh chords `⁷`, `⁶₅`, `⁴₃`, `⁴₂` (or `²`).
- **Chromatic chords**: `N⁶` (Neapolitan), `V⁷/V`, `vii°⁷/V`, `It⁺⁶ Fr⁺⁶ Ger⁺⁶`, modal mixture written with its real quality (e.g. `iv` in major, `♭VI`).
- **Non-chord tones**: PT (passing), NT (neighbor), APP (appoggiatura), ET (escape tone), SUS (suspension, with figures like 4–3, 7–6, 9–8, and 2–3 in the bass), RET (retardation), ANT (anticipation), PED (pedal).
- **Cadences**: PAC, IAC, HC, DC (deceptive), PC (plagal). Phrygian HC (iv⁶–V in minor).

## 3. Part-writing checklist (four-part chorale style)

Run every item when you write or check a chorale. Name each problem by beat and voice (e.g. "m. 3, beats 1–2, S and B: parallel 5ths, F5–C4 to G5–D4").

**Each chord**
- [ ] The spelling is correct and every required chord member is present. A complete triad can leave out the fifth when needed. A seventh chord can leave out the fifth.
- [ ] Doubling: usually the root in root position. Never double the leading tone or the chordal seventh (or any other tendency tone).
- [ ] Ranges: S C4–G5, A G3–D5, T C3–G4, B E2–D4 (courses vary a little).
- [ ] Spacing: S–A and A–T no more than an octave apart. T–B can be wider.
- [ ] No voice crossing.

**Each move between chords**
- [ ] No parallel 5ths or octaves (including unisons and compound forms) between any pair of voices.
- [ ] No contrary 5ths or octaves (P5 to P12 and the like), unless their course allows them.
- [ ] Direct (hidden) 5ths and octaves between the outer voices: approached by similar motion with a leap in the soprano. Avoid them.
- [ ] No voice overlap (a voice moving past the previous note of the neighboring voice).
- [ ] Leading tone resolves up to the tonic when it is in an outer voice (it may move down to the fifth in an inner voice in V–I).
- [ ] The chordal seventh resolves down by step.
- [ ] No augmented melodic intervals (e.g. A2 in harmonic minor between ♭6 and ♯7).
- [ ] Melodic leaps: avoid leaps larger than an octave, and any leap of a 7th. After a large leap, move by step in the opposite direction.

**The progression**
- [ ] The harmonic syntax makes sense: T → PD → D → T (I/vi → ii/IV → V/vii° → I).
- [ ] The cadence fits the assignment, and six-four chords are used only as cadential, passing, pedal (neighbor) or arpeggiated six-fours.

## 4. Analysis procedure

1. Find the key, and note the key signature as a separate fact. Check the opening and closing harmonies and the cadences.
2. Mark the cadences first, then the phrases.
3. Find the bass line and the harmonic rhythm.
4. Label the chords with Roman numerals and inversions. Mark non-chord tones.
5. Mark tonicizations and modulations (pivot chords: write both labels, e.g. `vi = ii`).
6. Name the form and the phrase structure.
7. Say what is musically interesting. A list of labels is not an analysis.

If the student sends a score, get the notes from it as described in score-input.md (exactly from MusicXML, or by a confirmed transcription from an image) before you analyze it. Reading notes from an image is error-prone.

## 5. Counterpoint (species) checklist

Fux-style two-voice species, as most courses teach it:

- **First species** (1:1): begin on a perfect consonance (P1, P5 or P8; P5 only when the counterpoint is above the cantus firmus) and end on P1 or P8. Use only consonances (P1, P5, P8, M/m3, M/m6, and compounds). The P4 is a dissonance against the bass. No parallel perfect intervals. Prefer contrary motion and stepwise lines with a single climax.
- **Second species** (2:1): the downbeat is consonant. The weak beat may be a dissonant passing tone.
- **Third species** (4:1): the first note of the bar is consonant. Passing and neighbor dissonances are allowed, as are the nota cambiata and the double neighbor.
- **Fourth species** (syncopated): suspensions prepared, held on the strong beat, and resolved down by step (7–6, 4–3 above; 2–3 below).
- **Fifth species** (florid): a mix of all of the above.

Details (e.g. whether the penultimate bar must be 6–8 or 3–1 at the cadence) depend on the textbook (Fux, Jeppesen, Salzer & Schachter, Schubert). Ask, or state your assumption.

## 6. Form vocabulary

- **Phrase level**: sentence (basic idea, repeated → continuation → cadence), period (antecedent with a weak cadence, consequent with a stronger one), hybrids (Caplin).
- **Small forms**: simple binary, rounded binary, ternary (ABA).
- **Large forms**: sonata (exposition: P, TR, S, closing. Development. Recapitulation. Optional coda), rondo (ABACA, ABACABA), theme and variations, sonata-rondo, minuet/scherzo and trio.
- **Hepokoski & Darcy (Sonata Theory)**: the medial caesura (MC), essential expositional closure (EEC), essential structural closure (ESC), rotations. Use these if their course uses them.
