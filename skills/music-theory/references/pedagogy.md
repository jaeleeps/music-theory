# Piano pedagogy

## 1. Accuracy rules

- **Never invent a citation, quotation, page number, or publication year.** If you are not sure of a source detail, say so and suggest how to check it (the library catalog, RILM, JSTOR, the book itself).
- Method books change between editions. Describe a method's approach, not the exact contents of a page.
- Musicology and music education writing usually uses **Chicago style** (notes-bibliography for musicology, author-date in some music-education venues). Some education programs use APA. Ask which one their program requires.

## 2. Method books and approaches

Know the approach well enough to compare methods, and be careful with specifics:

- **Faber, *Piano Adventures*** (Nancy and Randall Faber): widely used in North America. Starts from off-staff and pre-reading notation, combines landmark and intervallic reading, has a broad range of supplementary books, and includes adult and older-beginner series.
- **Alfred's** (*Basic Piano Library*, *Premier Piano Course*): Alfred's classic courses were long tied to the middle-C approach. *Premier* uses a broader range of positions.
- **Frances Clark, *The Music Tree*** (with Louise Goss and later Sam Holland): known for intervallic and landmark reading from the start. The Frances Clark Center and the *Piano Magazine* (formerly *Clavier Companion*) continue its work.
- **Bastien Piano Basics**: a multi-key approach (introduces many five-finger positions and keys early).
- **Suzuki Piano** (Shinichi Suzuki): rote, ear-first learning, the mother-tongue approach, parent involvement, listening to recordings, group and individual lessons. Reading is introduced later.
- **Dalcroze Eurhythmics** (Émile Jaques-Dalcroze): learning rhythm and musical understanding through movement, with solfège and improvisation.
- **Kodály** (Zoltán Kodály): singing, movable-do solfège, hand signs, rhythm syllables, folk song material. Mainly classroom-based.
- **Orff Schulwerk** (Carl Orff and Gunild Keetman): play, speech, movement, Orff instruments, improvisation.
- **Taubman approach** (Dorothy Taubman, continued by Edna Golandsky): rotation, alignment and coordinated motion, often discussed when preventing and recovering from injury.
- **Group piano and class piano**: digital piano labs, functional skills (harmonization, transposition, sight-reading, improvisation). Common in university courses for non-piano majors.

## 3. Reading approaches

| Approach | Idea | Typical strength | Typical critique |
|---|---|---|---|
| Middle C | Both thumbs share middle C. Notes added outward. | An easy start. | Students get stuck reading by finger number in one position. |
| Multi-key | Five-finger positions in many keys early. | Fluency with keys and transposition. | Reading by position over interval. |
| Intervallic / landmark | Read by direction and interval from guide notes. | Transfers to any position. Encourages reading by shape. | A slower start. Students need to know their landmarks. |

Most current methods combine these.

## 4. Learning theory often cited in pedagogy courses

- Jerome Bruner: the **spiral curriculum**. Enactive, iconic and symbolic representation (which maps to "sound before symbol").
- Lev Vygotsky: the **zone of proximal development**, scaffolding (the term "scaffolding" comes from Wood, Bruner & Ross, 1976).
- Benjamin Bloom: **Bloom's taxonomy** (revised by Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001), used for lesson objectives.
- Howard Gardner: multiple intelligences (popular, but its evidence base is disputed. Say so if the student relies on it).
- K. Anders Ericsson: **deliberate practice**.
- Edwin Gordon: **Music Learning Theory**, audiation.
- Carol Dweck: growth mindset (the replication evidence is mixed. Say so).
- Motor learning research: blocked vs. interleaved (random) practice, the contextual interference effect.

## 5. Lesson planning

A useful template for a 30–60 minute private lesson:

1. **Objectives**, stated as observable actions (Bloom verbs: identify, play, compare, improvise).
2. **Warm-up and technique**, tied to the repertoire (e.g. a D major scale before a D major piece).
3. **Repertoire work**: one concept per piece. Diagnose → model → guided practice → independent try.
4. **Musicianship**: sight-reading, theory, ear training, improvisation or composition. Short and regular.
5. **Assignment**: specific, measurable practice tasks ("hands separately, mm. 1–8, at ♩=60, 3 times a day"), not "practice the piece".
6. **Reflection and assessment**: what worked, and what to change next week.

Adjust for the student: young beginner (short activities, games, off-bench work), teen (autonomy, chosen repertoire), adult beginner (goals and constraints, explaining the why, coordination expectations), transfer student (diagnose gaps first), and students with disabilities (adapted materials and pacing; avoid assumptions).

## 6. Practice strategies

- Slow practice with a target tempo and steps up from it. Metronome ladders.
- Chunking: practice small sections, then link them. Practice starting from different points (start points / "islands").
- Hands separately, then together. Practice one voice, then add the others.
- Rhythmic variants for fast passages (dotted, reversed dotted, stop-and-go groupings).
- Mental practice and memory work: analytical memory (harmony and form), auditory, kinesthetic, and visual memory, so memory doesn't rest on one kind.
- Interleaving pieces and sections, rather than repeating one passage many times.
- Record and listen back. Self-assessment.
- Performance preparation: mock performances, managing performance anxiety.

## 7. Technique

- Scales and arpeggios, five-finger patterns, chords and cadences, technical studies (Czerny, Burgmüller, Hanon — Hanon's value is often debated), weight and arm use, rotation, wrist freedom, balancing voices within one hand, pedaling (syncopated/legato pedal, half pedal, una corda).
- **Do not list fingerings from memory as authoritative.** For standard scale fingerings, point the student to a technique book or their teacher, or work them out together and check them at the keyboard.
- Injury: unusual pain is a reason to stop and see a professional. Do not diagnose.

## 8. Levels and leveling systems

- **RCM (Royal Conservatory) Certificate Program**: Preparatory, then Levels 1–10, then ARCT.
- **ABRSM**: Initial, then Grades 1–8, then diplomas.
- **MTNA** (US) and state associations run competitions and certificate programs.
- **Repertoire leveling resources**: Jane Magrath, *The Pianist's Guide to Standard Teaching and Performance Literature* (levels 1–10). The current RCM and ABRSM syllabi.

Syllabi change. Tell the student to check the current edition.

## 9. Graduate written work

The student may ask for help with any of these:
- **Teaching philosophy statement**: help the student articulate their own beliefs with concrete examples from their teaching. Don't write a generic one for them.
- **Method review and comparison**: use consistent criteria (reading approach, pacing, repertoire, technique, supplementary materials, cultural range, cost).
- **Lesson plans and teaching observations**: use the template in section 5.
- **Literature review and research paper**: help with structure, argument, and finding search terms. Don't produce sources: the student finds them, and you help them read and use them.
- **Lecture-recital and program notes**: historical context, analysis, and the performer's perspective. Check dates and facts.
- **Comprehensive exam preparation**: quiz them, and use spaced review (see SKILL.md).
